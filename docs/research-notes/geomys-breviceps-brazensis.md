# How should _Geomys breviceps_ and _G. brazensis_ be classified?

_Jelle S. Zijlstra, July 2026_

[Bradley et al. (2023)](/a/63943) found two deeply divergent mitochondrial lineages
among pocket gophers traditionally placed in _Geomys breviceps_ and recognized them as
_G. breviceps_ and _G. brazensis_. The mitochondrial split is real, but the evidence
does not securely delimit two species or attach the correct available names to them. In
particular, the older name _sagittalis_ Merriam, 1895 may apply to the lineage called
_G. brazensis_, and a later nuclear-genomic study did not recover the proposed species
boundary.

## The available names

[Baird (1855)](/a/16646) described _Geomys breviceps_ from Prairie Mer Rouge, Morehouse
Parish, Louisiana. Its holotype is USNM:MAMM:156, an adult female skin and skull
collected in 1852. This name unquestionably applies to the northeastern Louisiana
lineage.

[Merriam (1895: 134)](/a/14560) described _G. breviceps sagittalis_ from “Clear Creek,
Galveston Bay, Texas.” Its holotype is
[USNM:MAMM:32936](http://n2t.net/ark:/65665/3102be915-881e-4889-a4f3-ff3288d2d3b8), an
adult male skin and skull collected by William Lloyd on 28 March 1892. Later authors
have variously placed the locality in Galveston County or southern Harris County;
Merriam did not specify a county. In either case, it is on the upper Texas coast near
western Galveston Bay.

[Davis (1938)](/a/12769) described _G. b. brazensis_ from 5 miles east of Kurten, Grimes
County, Texas (holotype TCWC:Mamm:537). [Davis (1940)](/a/30676) then recognized ten
geographic races as subspecies of _G. breviceps_. Seven bear directly on the present
problem: _breviceps_, _brazensis_, _dutcheri_, _terricolus_, _sagittalis_,
_pratincolus_, and _ludemani_. The other three have since been removed from this complex
in the classification used by [Bradley et al. (2023)](/a/63943): _attwateri_ is
recognized as a species, _ammophilus_ is treated as its synonym, and _texensis_ is also
recognized as a species. Thus, Davis's _G. breviceps_ was broader than either the
traditional late-twentieth-century concept or the _breviceps_–_brazensis_ problem
considered here. Among the five names Davis introduced, _dutcheri_ was based on Fort
Gibson, Oklahoma (USNM:MAMM:64591), _terricolus_ on north of Texas City (TCWC:Mamm:624),
_pratincolus_ on east of Liberty (TCWC:Mamm:1128), _ludemani_ on southwest of Fannett
(TCWC:Mamm:1135), and _ammophilus_ on Cuero. Davis's spelling _pratincolus_ was later
unnecessarily changed to _pratincola_.

Davis treated soil and river barriers as the main causes of differentiation. His
_dutcheri_ ranged through the Arkansas River valley from Tulsa east at least to Benton,
Arkansas, and south into northeastern Texas and northwestern Louisiana. He reported
intergradation between _dutcheri_ and _brazensis_ between the Sabine and Trinity rivers.
He restricted _sagittalis_ to a small coastal area around Clear Creek and Galveston Bay,
separated from nearby populations by unsuitable clay soils. Thus, his names denoted
geographically localized, morphologically diagnosed populations, not the mitochondrial
lineages later recovered by Bradley et al.

“Clear Creek” is treated as the historical settlement now called League City, not as the
mouth of the present watercourse. This is supported by the collection date (1892), by
near-topotypes from League City listed by [Schmidly et al. (2023)](/a/63946), and by the
[Galveston County Museum's history of League City](https://www.galvestoncountytx.gov/county-offices/county-museum/community-histories/league-city/),
which records that the post office was called Clear Creek until 1896.

For molecular specimens, coordinates printed in the papers or supplied by the TTU and
LSUMZ catalogues through GBIF were preferred. Verbal localities were georeferenced from
named places and offsets. There is one significant source problem:
[Halsey et al. (2025)](/a/70516) printed coordinates near 28.9°N, 99.06°W for TK48935
and TK48940, although both are catalogued from the Quitman area in Wood County, about
500 km away. The TTU catalogue repeats those bad coordinates for TK48935 and TK48939 but
gives 32.7970°N, 95.4090°W for TK48940 at the same catalogued locality. The map uses the
latter point for all three and flags the correction in the coordinate table.

The geographic pattern makes the sampling limitations conspicuous. Neither recent study
included material from the type locality of _sagittalis_, _ludemani_, _pratincolus_, or
_terricolus_. Bradley et al. sampled near the type localities of _breviceps_,
_brazensis_, and _dutcheri_; Halsey et al. sampled near those of _brazensis_ and
_dutcheri_. Every molecular specimen called _sagittalis_ came from well inland and
mostly far north of Clear Creek. These are samples of the broad 1979 taxonomic concept
of _sagittalis_, not of its name-bearing population.

## Taxonomic history

[Baker & Glass (1951)](/a/13230) placed _breviceps_ and all of Davis's subspecies in _G.
bursarius_. Their evidence was a 30-mile transect east of Norman, Oklahoma, along which
characters attributed to _G. bursarius major_ and _G. breviceps dutcheri_ intergraded.
They nevertheless continued to list _breviceps_, _brazensis_, _dutcheri_, _pratincolus_,
_sagittalis_, _terricolus_, and _ludemani_ as separate subspecies.

[Hall (1981)](/a/35036) retained these traditional subspecies within _G. bursarius_. His
map 310 (p. 502) is close to Davis's arrangement: the area later mapped as “_Geomys
species novum_” by Bradley et al. is included in _dutcheri_, while the upper Texas coast
is divided among _ludemani_, _sagittalis_, and _terricolus_. Hall also retained
_brazensis_ and _pratincola_ separately.

[Honeycutt & Schmidly (1979)](/a/72981) revised Texas _Geomys_ based on karyotypes and
morphology. They studied chromosomes and 12 cranial measurements and found no
morphological or karyotypic separation among samples called _brazensis_, _dutcheri_,
_ludemani_, _pratincolus_, _sagittalis_, and _terricolus_. They assigned all six to
chromosomal race E (2n = 74, FN = 70) and used _sagittalis_, the oldest of those six
names, for the combined subspecies. They kept the isolated Mer Rouge population as _G.
bursarius breviceps_ because it averaged larger, even though its karyotype was the same.
Their broad _sagittalis_ therefore extended from the Brazos River through eastern Texas
and western Louisiana to Arkansas and eastern Oklahoma.

[Bohlin & Zimmerman (1982)](/a/72982) used allozyme data from 37 populations to show
that chromosomal races D and E maintained largely separate gene pools, with one apparent
F1 hybrid in a contact zone near Norman. They elevated race E back to species rank as
_G. breviceps_, while retaining the broad subspecific arrangement inherited from
Honeycutt & Schmidly: _G. breviceps breviceps_ in Mer Rouge only, and _G. breviceps
sagittalis_ broadly distributed in Oklahoma, Arkansas, Texas, and Louisiana. This
arrangement was followed by [Sulentich et al. (1991)](/a/40057) and by the
[Handbook of the Mammals of the World (2016)](/a/59599).

This history explains why specimens far from the type locality of _sagittalis_ were
routinely catalogued under that name. Those identifications do not show that the animals
are consubspecific with the name-bearing population at Clear Creek.

## The mitochondrial split of Bradley et al. (2023)

[Bradley et al. (2023)](/a/63943) analyzed cytochrome _b_. Within the traditional _G.
breviceps_, they recovered two principal clades separated by an average Kimura
two-parameter distance of 8.81%:

- one contained samples identified as _breviceps_, _dutcheri_, and _sagittalis_ in part
  (ten localities across Texas, Louisiana, Arkansas, and Oklahoma);
- the other contained _brazensis_, _pratincolus_, and _sagittalis_ in part (three
  localities, all in the interior of Texas).

They sequenced specimens from or near the type localities of _breviceps_, _brazensis_,
and _dutcheri_, but not from the type locality of _sagittalis_, _ludemani_, or
_terricolus_. Their _sagittalis_ samples came from scattered inland localities in Texas,
Louisiana, and Arkansas that had been assigned to the broad 1979 concept. Those samples
occurred in both mitochondrial clades. This result demonstrates that the broad
_sagittalis_ was composite, but it says nothing direct about the mitochondrial identity
of the name-bearing population at Clear Creek.

Bradley et al. called the two major clades _G. breviceps_ and _G. brazensis_. They
retained _brazensis_ and _pratincola_ as subspecies of the latter and placed some former
“_sagittalis_” samples there. Within _G. breviceps_, they recognized _breviceps_,
_dutcheri_, and _sagittalis_ in part, but suggested that some of the latter material
would require a “_G. breviceps novum_.” Figure 9 instead maps the units as _G. breviceps
breviceps_ (6), _G. breviceps dutcheri_ (7), _G. breviceps sagittalis_ (8), and “_G.
species novum_” (19). The 2024 Texas checklist by [Schmidly et al. (2024)](/a/67259)
adopted this arrangement, recognizing _G. brazensis_ but retaining the coastal Clear
Creek population as _G. breviceps sagittalis_. They claim _G. breviceps_ has a disjunct
distribution, but there is no genetic evidence that the coastal Texas populations are
closer to _G. breviceps_ than the geographically adjacent _G. brazensis_.

There is no molecular data from the region in southeastern Texas that holds the type
localities of _sagittalis_, _ludemani_, _terricolus_, and _pratincolus_ (the sample
identified as _pratincolus_ is from 74 km north of the type locality). If these
populations are in fact part of the adjacent _brazensis_ clade, then _Geomys breviceps
sagittalis_ Merriam, 1895, would have priority over _Geomys breviceps brazensis_ Davis,
1938, as the name of the species.

Bradley et al. (2023) also support continued recognition of various subspecies and
suggest that some populations previously assigned to _sagittalis_ represent an unnamed
subspecies (or species, according to their map legend). However, their molecular data
(Fig. 3b) shows little apparent geographic structure within each clade.

## Nuclear genomic evidence

[Halsey et al. (2025)](/a/70516) analyzed tens of thousands of nuclear SNPs from
ddRADseq data. Their operational taxonomic unit VIII combined samples labelled _G.
breviceps sagittalis_, _G. bursarius brazensis_, and _G. bursarius dutcheri_. (This
nomenclature mixes species names in a confusing way. No author has suggested that
_sagittalis_ is part of _breviceps_ while _brazensis_ and _dutcheri_ remain part of
_bursarius_.) The _brazensis_ series was from 5.81 miles east of Kurten and the
_dutcheri_ series from Fort Gibson, so both closely represent their name-bearing
populations. In contrast, the five purported _sagittalis_ specimens came from two widely
separated areas, not Clear Creek. TK24932, from near Maysfield in Milam County, was
collected about 33 km south of Bradley et al.'s clade-I samples at Reagan and 65 km west
of the type locality of _brazensis_. The other four were from the Quitman area in Wood
County; one of them, TK48934, has exactly the same stated locality as Bradley et al.'s
clade-II specimen TTU69299. Thus, the samples pooled under _sagittalis_ probably span
both sides of Bradley et al.'s mitochondrial division.

Halsey et al. did not report results for the Milam and Wood County specimens separately.
They treated all five as one _sagittalis_ group in the STRUCTURE, phylogenetic, and
pairwise-differentiation summaries. The pooled group averaged 215,464 recovered loci and
had 6,203 private alleles; its reported *F*ₛₜ was 0.157 from _brazensis_ and 0.205 from
_dutcheri_, while the value between _brazensis_ and _dutcheri_ was 0.278. Their tree
placed pooled _sagittalis_ with _brazensis_, with _dutcheri_ as their sister, and all
three were combined in OTU VIII. Because the geographically informative specimens were
pooled, these results cannot show whether the Milam animal resembles clade I while the
Wood County animals resemble clade II, or whether nuclear similarity actually crosses
the mitochondrial boundary. The study therefore neither independently corroborates nor
directly refutes Bradley et al.'s split. Reporting is further complicated by
inconsistent sample totals for _brazensis_ and _dutcheri_: Appendix I lists eight and
five, respectively, whereas the supplementary locus table gives 12 and seven.

[Álvarez-Castañeda & Segura-Trujillo (2025)](/a/68853) also did not recognize _G.
brazensis_, listing _G. breviceps_ with the subspecies _breviceps_, _ozarkensis_, and
_sagittalis_. Their genus-level review did not discuss the conflict in detail, and its
transfer of _ozarkensis_ from _G. bursarius_ is itself unexplained, so it does not
resolve the problem.

## Assessment

The evidence supports deep mitochondrial structure within the traditional _Geomys
breviceps_, but not the currently adopted division into _G. breviceps_ and _G.
brazensis_. The 2023 study used a single mitochondrial locus, inherited locality
identifications from an explicitly broad concept of _sagittalis_, and lacked samples
from the most nomenclaturally important type locality. The 2025 nuclear study pooled
five specimens called _sagittalis_ that probably came from opposite sides of the
mitochondrial division, preventing an individual-level test of that boundary.

Pending an integrative revision, the conservative course is to retain a single species,
_G. breviceps_, and treat its subspecific limits as unresolved. If two species are
ultimately supported, their names cannot be settled without assigning the Clear Creek
population—and ideally the holotype of _sagittalis_—to a lineage. Ancient DNA from
USNM:MAMM:32936 may be particularly useful because the Clear Creek population is
reported to be extirpated. Dense nuclear and mitochondrial sampling around the type
localities of _breviceps_, _sagittalis_, _brazensis_, _dutcheri_, _pratincolus_,
_ludemani_, and _terricolus_, together with direct morphological comparison of their
types, is needed to clarify the boundaries of any proposed species and their names.
