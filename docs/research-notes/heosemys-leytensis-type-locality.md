# Was the type series of _Heosemys leytensis_ really from Leyte?

_Jelle S. Zijlstra, August 2026_

_Heosemys leytensis_ Taylor, 1920 (now _Siebenrockiella leytensis_) was described from
two turtles said to have been collected at Cabalian in southern Leyte. The two original
specimens were subsequently lost, but a third old specimen with the same stated
locality, CAS 60930, was designated as the neotype by [Buskirk (1989)](/a/73173).
Natural populations were found much later near Taytay on northern Palawan and on nearby
Dumaran Island ([Diesmos et al., 2004](/a/73172)). The current consensus is that the
Leyte locality was erroneous and that all three old specimens came from the Palawan
region.

Morphology strongly supports identifying the Palawan turtles as Taylor's species.
Reconstructing the origin of the old specimens is harder: their attribution to the
Palawan region rests on distributional evidence and the collector's history.

## Is the Palawan population _Heosemys leytensis_?

[Diesmos et al. (2004)](/a/73172) examined live and captive Palawan turtles and reported
the same unusual combination of characters described by Taylor and visible in his
illustrations: the shell proportions and serrated anterior rim, narrow plastron,
characteristic gular and anal notches, enlarged forelimb scales, reddish-brown
coloration, and pale transverse line behind the head. They also documented wild turtles
in streams and adjacent forest near Lake Manguao at Taytay and on Dumaran. Their
measurements included 24 animals, four of them observed in the wild on Dumaran.

Buskirk compared CAS 60930 in detail with Taylor's description and illustrations and
regarded its identity as unquestionable. It shares the most distinctive plastral and
head characters. Buskirk nevertheless listed minor differences: for example, three
rather than four enlarged forelimb scales, weaker marginal serration, somewhat larger
axillary and inguinal scutes, and a parabolic rather than semicircular anal notch.

[Diesmos et al. (2005)](/a/73174) directly examined CAS 60930, a preserved Palawan
specimen (PNM 8488), and an additional 47 living turtles. They illustrated the neotype
beside a Palawan animal and diagnosed the taxon by a strongly projecting front of the
plastron, ginkgo-leaf-shaped vertebral scutes 2–4, absence of the temporal arch, and
pale lines behind the head. The neotype itself lacks the second vertebral scute,
limiting that particular comparison, but the other characters link it to the Palawan
series. No other Philippine turtle is known to combine these features.

Diesmos et al. sequenced mitochondrial cytochrome _b_ and the nuclear R35 intron from
one specimen, PNM 8488, apparently purchased from a Palawan wildlife trader. The
sequences strongly placed that turtle as the sister lineage of _Siebenrockiella
crassicollis_, supporting its transfer to _Siebenrockiella_. Only the Palawan specimen
was sequenced; conspecificity with Taylor's types rests on the morphological evidence. I
found no published DNA comparison involving any of the three old specimens.

## The two original specimens

[Taylor (1920)](/a/73180) introduced the species. He wrote that Gregorio Lopez collected
two specimens in southern Leyte together with other turtles intended for dissection in
the zoological department of the University of the Philippines. Lopez had obtained 40
turtles belonging to three species: _Cyclemys dhor_ (the animals later discussed as _C.
dentata_), _C. amboinensis_ (now _Cuora amboinensis_), and _H. leytensis_. Taylor
inferred from the number of specimens that these turtles were plentiful there.

Taylor designated an unnumbered University of the Philippines specimen from “Cabalian,
southern Leyte” as the type and identified Lopez as its collector. It was an adult male
with a carapace length of 210 mm. His second specimen was from the same locality, had a
carapace length of 126 mm, and was described only as medium-sized. Taylor did not state
its sex. Later authors have called the second specimen a juvenile or a female; only the
former is compatible with Taylor's account. The two are now usually treated as the
holotype and paratype, although Buskirk called them cotypes.

Taylor described and illustrated a distinctive turtle. Its relevant features included a
mostly unkeeled, reddish-brown carapace with serrated anterior margins; a narrow
plastron; deep notches between the gulars and between the gular and humeral scutes; a
deep rounded anal notch; three or four enlarged scales on each forelimb; webbed digits;
and a narrow yellow line across the head behind the ears. The second specimen showed
some age-related differences, including more conspicuous pale markings on the head.

In a later catalogue of type specimens, [Taylor (1944)](/a/49757) recorded the
unnumbered holotype as having passed from the University of the Philippines to the
Bureau of Science. He again mentioned Cabalian, Leyte, and Gregorio Lopez, but stated
that the collection date was unknown. He did not mention the second specimen. The types
are generally believed to have been destroyed with the Bureau of Science collections
during the 1945 Battle of Manila.

## What do we know about Gregorio Lopez?

I found no biography of Lopez. Specimen records and comments by his colleagues provide
some details of his life. The earliest source found that names him is Taylor's original
description of _Heosemys leytensis_. The journal volume is dated February 1920 but says
the paper was issued on 29 May. By then Lopez had already supplied the 40-turtle lot
from which Taylor selected the two _H. leytensis_. The
[Bureau of Science report for 1920](https://archive.org/details/acw9612.0019.001.umich.edu)
mentions an unnamed fish collector, but gives no basis for identifying that person as
Lopez. No documentation found so far securely places Lopez at the Bureau, or collecting
for Taylor, before the turtle lot.

The [report for 1921](https://archive.org/details/acw9612.0020.001.umich.edu) states
that cooperation among Taylor, R. E. Dickerson, and the California Academy of Sciences
made funds available for all or part of Lopez's field expenses in Romblon, Sibuyan,
Culion, the Batan Islands, and elsewhere.

The [report for 1922](https://archive.org/details/acw9612.0020.001.umich.edu) gives a
detailed itinerary. Lopez worked around Monja Island, Mariveles, Nasugbu, and Jamillo;
in May he collected near **Cabalian, Leyte**, on Dinagat, and in Surigao; in September
and October he worked in the **Calamian Islands**; and in November and December he
returned to **Leyte** and visited the Camotes. The report calls his collections
extensive and especially valuable and says that, in addition to fishes, he obtained
reptiles, amphibians, mollusks, and crustaceans.

[Taylor (1925)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/25/The_Philippine_journal_of_science_%28IA_act3868.0026.001.umich.edu%29.pdf)
called him a Bureau of Science collector who had made many excellent collections across
the Philippines. In naming _Mindorogobius lopezi_,
[Herre (1945)](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/db/Proceedings_of_the_Biological_Society_of_Washington_%28IA_proceedings5859194546biol%29.pdf)
described Gregorio Lopez as a fisherman, an exceptional linguist, and his field
collector and companion of many years.

## The third specimen and the CAS ledger

[Buskirk (1989)](/a/73173) reported an adult female, CAS 60930, received by the CAS on
24 December 1922. The specimen's entry occurred at the end of a catalogue page of
amphibians and reptiles from “Calabian” (presumably a typographic error for Cabalian),
Leyte, mostly attributed to Lopez. The locality was carried into the CAS record, but the
collector column on the _H. leytensis_ row did not name Lopez. It instead bore a faint
note, “Exc. Univ. of Philippines”; the original number was 2385. Buskirk inferred that
Lopez probably collected the specimen with Taylor's types or within the following two
years, but acknowledged that Taylor apparently did not know of it. He designated CAS
60930 as the neotype.

The digitized
[CAS catalogue spread](https://archive.org/details/catalogueofdepar11unse/page/n67/mode/2up)
confirms Buskirk's account. CAS 60903–60930 are all assigned to Cabalian by ditto marks.
Lopez is entered as collector for most of the series, which includes frogs, lizards, and
four _Cuora amboinensis_ as well as _H. leytensis_. On the last row, however, the
institutional-exchange notation replaces the collector's name. The
[current CAS record for CAS 60930](https://researcharchive.calacademy.org/research/herpetology/catalog/index.asp?xAction=getrec&close=true&CatalogNo=CAS+60930)
likewise leaves the collector blank.

### What can be recovered from original no. 2385?

The manuscript CAS ledger calls 2385 the “Orig. No.”; the current CAS interface labels
the column “Collector's (Field) Number.” The same number range covers material credited
to Lopez, Taylor, Francis Link, and Richard McGregor. The current CAS record for no.
2260 renders it as “EHT-2260” (i.e., Edward H. Taylor's personal collection). I use
“source number” here: probably a University of the Philippines, Bureau of Science, or
Taylor collection number retained when the specimens went to CAS. The sequence may
reflect processing order; its relationship to collection dates is uncertain.

A current CAS search yields 56 records attributed exactly to G. F. Lopez, representing
41 distinct source-number strings between 1192 and 2404, in addition to CAS 60930 with
its blank collector field. Those records and two manuscript catalogue spreads allow part
of the sequence to be reconstructed:

Specimens from Leyte, the Calamian Islands, and elsewhere were interspersed in the
sequence. Recovering entries immediately adjacent to 2385 might help assess its
assignment to Cabalian.

The consignment received on 24 December 1922 combined material from Itbayat, Luzon,
Leyte, Dinagat, Coron, and Busuanga, some collected at least thirteen months earlier.
Lopez's September–October 1922 Calamian work was followed by November–December work on
Leyte and the Camotes. Material from these trips could have reached the institution in
close succession, allowing a label or source-number mix-up. The itinerary also allows a
Leyte origin for CAS 60930; its accession date and source number leave both
possibilities open.

### Were other specimens really from Leyte?

Taylor's publications corroborate several Cabalian records. In a
[second 1922 paper](https://biostor.org/reference/110935), Taylor separately reported 18
_Staurois natator_ from Cabalian collected by Lopez and compared their coloration with
his Zamboanga and Basilan specimens. Seven of those frogs survive at CAS under source
nos. 1192 and 1908–1913. This frog is apparently endemic to the Greater Mindanao faunal
region of the Philippines. In the same paper Taylor compared a Lopez _Megophrys
stejnegeri_ from Cabalian—apparently CAS 60903, no. 2326—with specimens from Surigao and
Zamboanga. The catalogue also contains Leyte _Platymantis corrugatus_ nos. 2338–2339, a
species Taylor considered absent from the Palawan and Sulu groups, and coherent runs of
Cabalian geckos and skinks at nos. 2269–2292.

These records suggest that any locality error affected one or more turtles within a
largely authentic Cabalian lot.

### Could the original ledger still exist?

I found no digitized University of the Philippines or Bureau of Science field ledger
that resolves no. 2385. The institutional record has a major gap: the Bureau of Science
building and virtually the entire national natural-history collection were destroyed in
the Battle of Manila in February 1945
([National Museum of the Philippines](https://www.nationalmuseum.gov.ph/about-us/history/)).
Any source catalogue kept only there was probably lost.

The
[Edward H. Taylor papers at the University of Kansas](https://archives.lib.ku.edu/repositories/3/resources/3147)
comprise 43 boxes and explicitly include field catalogues and specimen data. The online
finding aid is not detailed enough to identify a Philippine catalogue covering nos.
2260–2404, and the relevant material is not digitized. An archival search at the Kenneth
Spencer Research Library may locate no. 2385 in a pre-CAS ledger, label, or exchange
list. CAS accession correspondence is another possible source, but I found no public
scan or finding-aid entry for the December 1922 University of the Philippines exchange.

## Evidence for and against Leyte

The affirmative evidence for Leyte consists of Taylor's detailed 1920 account, repeated
in 1944, and the Cabalian assignment of CAS 60930 in the CAS ledger. The original
specimens could have represented a rare or subsequently extinct Leyte population.

Several independent observations make that explanation unlikely:

- [Diesmos et al. (2004)](/a/73172) found natural populations on northern Palawan and
  Dumaran.
- Searches around Cabalian by Taylor, later Silliman University collectors, Alcala, and
  Diesmos and colleagues produced no additional specimens. Interviews reported by
  Diesmos et al. also failed to elicit descriptions of a matching local turtle.
- Taylor's same alleged Cabalian turtle collection included _Cyclemys dentata_. Modern
  reviews treat this as another erroneous Leyte record because the species is otherwise
  known in the Philippines from the Palawan and Sulu regions and has likewise not been
  found again on Leyte ([Diesmos et al., 2008](/a/73171)). Its presence suggests that
  Palawan-region turtles entered the lot attributed to Cabalian.
- Lopez demonstrably collected herpetological specimens on both Leyte and the
  Palawan–Calamian side of the country. The interleaved source numbers in material
  received by the CAS on the same date make a curatorial mix-up possible.
- Palawan belongs to a biogeographic region with strong Sundaic affinities, whereas
  Leyte belongs to the eastern Philippine island complex. A narrowly distributed forest
  turtle occurring naturally in both regions but nowhere between them would be unusual.

[Diesmos et al. (2012)](/a/73175) characterized the Leyte record as a mislabeling. They
said the types most likely came from either northern Palawan **or the Calamian group**,
but selected “northern Palawan in the Province of Palawan, Philippines” because that was
where the species had been confirmed in the wild.

## Assessment

The three old specimens probably came from the Palawan region, although their recorded
Leyte provenance remains unresolved. The similar names “Cabalian” and “Calamian”, and
the form “Calabian” in the CAS ledger, suggest a possible source of confusion.

[Diesmos et al., 2008](/a/73171) specifically selected northern Palawan as the type
locality, but it appears more likely that the neotype came from the Calamianes. Although
the species has not been recorded there in modern times, these islands are close and
biogeographically connected to Palawan, and Lopez is known to have collected there
around the time the neotype must have been obtained. For the original two specimens, the
Calamian hypothesis is weaker because they predate Lopez's first documented
western-Philippine work, at Culion in 1921, and have no surviving numbers.
