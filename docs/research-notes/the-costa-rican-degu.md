# The Costa Rican degu

_Jelle S. Zijlstra, October 2026_

In 1869, Alexander von Frantzius reported a degu from Turrialba, Costa Rica. Trouessart
subsequently named this animal _Octodon degus frantziusi_. The specimen was lost in
transit to Europe, and its identity rests on Frantzius's brief description. Alston
suggested _Nyctomys_; Tate later called it a geomyid. What evidence supports these
interpretations?

## History

[Frantzius (1869:275–276)](/a/72294) received the specimen from “Turialba” in
September 1859. It reportedly had been knocked down from a tree. He sent it to Europe,
but it was lost in transit. He identified it as _Octodon degus_ after subsequently
examining degus in collections and comparing illustrations with his recollection of the
animal.

[Alston (1880:168–169)](/a/59879) questioned the record. After translating the
description, he wrote that it “might apply as well to a Hesperomys of the subgenus
Nyctomys as to an Octodon.” The relevant installment of the _Biologia
Centrali-Americana_ appeared in October 1880, although the completed mammal volume bears
an 1882 title-page date.

[Trouessart (1881:175)](/a/36771) placed Frantzius's _degus_ in the synonymy of
_Cumingii_, variety _peruana_, within his _Octodon degus_ entry, listing Costa Rica
alongside the Frantzius citation. In the next catalogue,
[Trouessart (1897:601)](/a/59285) separated the Costa Rican reference as a doubtful form
“b.—? degus,” distinct from “a.—peruanus.” Finally, [Trouessart (1904:500)](/a/59289)
supplied “b.—? frantziusi, nom. nov.” under _Octodon degus_, again citing Frantzius and
Costa Rica. He supplied no new specimen or descriptive evidence. The name appeared in
the 1904 installment of the supplement, not in 1905 as the completed volume's date might
suggest.

[Alfaro (1897:41, footnote)](/a/37835) also discussed the record. He reiterated
Frantzius's 1859 specimen from Turrialba, adding the name “rata de árbol” and citing the
account in León Fernández's _Documentos_, volume I, page 399.

[Tate (1935:379)](/a/10086) took a different view: Frantzius had misidentified “what was
probably a geomyid.” In his account of Trouessart's later name, Tate treated the animal
as a geomyid and urged workers on Geomyidae to consider the name. He gave no anatomical
argument or new specimen evidence for that placement. He also rendered the name as
_franziusi_; the original spelling is _frantziusi_. Among the treatments examined here,
Alston and Tate offer the competing identifications; Trouessart formalizes the name,
while Alfaro repeats the original record.

## Frantzius's account

The complete entry from [Frantzius (1869:275–276)](/a/72294) follows. Line breaks and
line-end hyphenation have been normalized; the wording and historical spelling are
retained.

> Fam. Octodontina Waterh. Gattung Octodon Benn. Octodon Degus Waterh. Waterhouse,
> Mammalia II, 253, Tb. II, fig. 2. v. Tschudi, Fauna Peruana p. 171. Tb. 12.
>
> Ein Exemplar, welches ich im September 1859 von Turialba erhielt und welches dort von
> einem Baum heruntergeschlagen worden war, wurde zwar nach Europa geschickt, doch ist
> dasselbe auf dem Transport verunglückt, so dass ich bei der Artbestimmung dieser
> Baumratte nur auf die kurze Notiz beschränkt bin, welche ich über dieselbe
> zurückbehalten hatte. Der gepinselte Schwanz, der Kuppennagel der Daumen der
> Vorderfüsse lassen keinen Zweifel, dass es eine Octodonart ist. Die Farbe des weichen
> Pelzes war oben graubraun, die Unterseite heller, die Körperlänge betrug 4½ Zoll, die
> des Schwanzes ebensoviel, die Vibrissen hatten eine Länge von 2½ Zoll, die Ohren waren
> gross und nackt und ragten über den Scheitel hinaus.
>
> Nachdem ich Gelegenheit gehabt habe, in verschiedenen Sammlungen den O. Degus zu sehen
> und das mir in der Erinnerung gebliebene Bild mit verschiedenen Abbildungen zu
> vergleichen, scheint mir der in Costarica vorkommende Octodon unzweifelhaft der O.
> Degus Waterh. zu sein.

Translation:

> Family Octodontina Waterh. Genus Octodon Benn. Octodon Degus Waterh. Waterhouse,
> Mammalia II, 253, plate II, fig. 2. von Tschudi, Fauna Peruana p. 171, plate 12.
>
> A specimen that I received from Turialba in September 1859, and that had been knocked
> down from a tree there, was indeed sent to Europe, but it was lost in transit, so that
> in determining the species of this tree rat I am restricted to the short note about it
> that I had retained. The brush-tipped tail and the rounded nail on the thumbs of the
> forefeet leave no doubt that it is a species of Octodon. The soft fur was gray-brown
> above, with the underside lighter; the body length was 4½ Zoll, the tail just as long,
> and the vibrissae were 2½ Zoll long. The ears were large and naked and projected above
> the crown of the head.
>
> Having had the opportunity to see O. Degus in various collections and to compare the
> image remaining in my memory with various illustrations, it seems to me that the
> Octodon occurring in Costa Rica is unquestionably O. Degus Waterh.

“Zoll” is retained because the measurement standard is unspecified; a Zoll was about 2.5
cm. “Rounded nail” translates _Kuppennagel_, a nail covering the fingertip rather than a
pointed claw. “Lost in transit” translates _auf dem Transport verunglückt_; Frantzius
does not explain the accident. In [Alston's (1880:168)](/a/59879) translation, the
clause describing the large, naked ears is omitted and the phrase about projecting above
the head is attached to the whiskers. The German unambiguously refers to the ears.

## Assessment

### A geomyid is unlikely

The strongest objections to Tate's interpretation are the ears and tail. The regional
geomyids described by [Goldman (1920)](/a/9520) have ears reduced to skin folds and a
short, smooth, naked tail. These are poor matches for large ears projecting above the
crown and a brush-tipped tail as long as the body. A capture in a tree also conflicts
with their burrowing habits. Soft brown fur alone would not exclude a geomyid, and
unmentioned cheek pouches or digging claws cannot be treated as observed absences.
Nevertheless, the positively described features make a geomyid identification difficult
to sustain.

### _Nyctomys_ is a good candidate

_Nyctomys sumichrasti_ combines arboreal habits, soft fur, long vibrissae, and a hairy
tail with a terminal brush. [Hunt et al. (2004:1–2)](/a/2294) report mean head-and-body
lengths of 118 mm and mean tail lengths of 113–115 mm in Nicaraguan adults: the
proportions agree closely with Frantzius's equal body and tail measurements. The
absolute size is also broadly compatible with 4½ historical inches. The same account
describes young Costa Rican and Panamanian animals as gray-brown and woolly until nearly
adult size, providing a plausible match for the recorded pelage without requiring the
warmer coloration usual in adults.

The fit is incomplete. [Hunt et al. (2004:1)](/a/2294) describe the ears as small, not
large. The characteristic dark eye rings are not mentioned. Nor does the forethumb nail
establish _Octodon_: the account lacks the dentition and detailed cranial or pedal
characters needed to test the generic identification. Alston's suggestion is therefore
persuasive as a candidate, but the surviving description does not demonstrate that the
animal was _Nyctomys_.

### Other candidates

- **_Ototylomys phyllotis_** is an arboreal alternative with conspicuous ears and
  gray-brown pelage in Costa Rica. Its virtually hairless, scaly tail is a poor match
  for the recorded brush, and adults are generally larger than the animal described
  ([Lawlor, 1982:1](/a/40176)).
- **_Diplomys labilis_** is the more plausible echimyid alternative: it is arboreal,
  lacks spines, and has a hairy tail. Records from Costa Rica and southeastern Nicaragua
  make it geographically worth considering. However, its small ears, much larger usual
  size, and short tail hairs rather than a distinct terminal pencil fit less well. A
  Nicaraguan specimen measured 225 mm in head-and-body length, 240 mm in tail length,
  and 16 mm in ear length ([Emmons and Patton, 2015:890–893](/a/25382);
  [Martínez-Fonseca et al., 2018:555–556](/a/38469)).
- **_Hoplomys gymnurus_** occurs on the Caribbean slope of Costa Rica, but its largely
  terrestrial habits, short rounded ears, relatively short and nearly naked tail, and
  coarse spines make it a substantially poorer fit. Invoking a very young animal could
  reduce the size objection but would not explain the brush-tipped tail or prominent
  ears. [Handley (1959:2)](/a/43485) describes spines in juveniles as well as adults;
  small size alone is not evidence for a soft-furred _Hoplomys_
  ([Patton and Emmons, 2015:940–942](/a/25394)).

The combination of a terminal tail brush, nearly equal body and tail lengths, soft
gray-brown fur, long whiskers, and reported tree capture favors _Nyctomys_ among these
candidates. None of the later historical treatments supplies independent anatomical
evidence. The defensible conclusion is an unidentified Costa Rican rodent, plausibly
_Nyctomys_. Retaining _frantziusi_ as a name of doubtful application is appropriate
unless further original documentation is recovered.
