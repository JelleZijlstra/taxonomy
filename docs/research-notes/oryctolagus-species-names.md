# How many species of _Oryctolagus_ are there, and what are their names?

_Jelle S. Zijlstra, July 2026_

Among living rabbits, the evidence supports **two species of _Oryctolagus_**. One is the
southwestern Iberian lineage usually called _O. c. algirus_ (Oca), and the other is the
northeastern Iberian lineage, including French, domestic, and most introduced rabbits,
usually called _O. c. cuniculus_ (Occ). Their recognition as species is more convincing
than their conventional names. The usual combination—_Oryctolagus algirus_ for Oca and
_O. cuniculus_ for Occ—is plausible and may prove correct, but it has not yet been
established by a nomenclatural revision that connects both lineages to name-bearing
types.

## Oca and Occ in the Iberian Peninsula

The first molecular surveys found two deeply divergent mitochondrial lineages.
[Biju-Duval et al. (1991)](/a/73031) sampled southern and northern Spain, France,
domestic rabbits, and Zembra in Tunisia. Their lineage A occurred in southern Spain,
whereas lineage B occurred in northern Spain, France, domestic rabbits, and Zembra. They
estimated about 4% mitochondrial divergence and a split around 2 Ma. Denser Iberian
sampling by [Branco et al. (2000)](/a/72986) and [Branco et al. (2002)](/a/73030)
recovered the now familiar southwest– northeast division and a contact zone running
northwest to southeast across the peninsula.

This is not merely a mitochondrial division. Protein loci, autosomal markers, the X
chromosome, and the Y chromosome recover the same two historical population groups.
[Ferrand & Branco (2007)](/a/73017) found that 15.1% of allozyme diversity was
attributable to the division between them. Studies of X-linked loci found restricted and
strongly heterogeneous introgression ([Geraldes et al., 2006](/a/73000);
[Carneiro et al., 2010](/a/72995)), and the Y chromosome has an especially steep
transition ([Geraldes et al., 2008](/a/72991)). Genome-wide studies show a permeable
genomic background interrupted by sharply differentiated regions, especially near
centromeres and on the X chromosome ([Carneiro et al., 2009](/a/72988);
[Carneiro et al., 2013](/a/72997); [Carneiro et al., 2014](/a/72998)). The clines are
not all coincident and gene flow is asymmetric, but the hybrid zone remains
geographically narrow and contains genomic regions that resist introgression
([Rafati et al., 2018](/a/73033)). Hybridization therefore does not erase the evidence
that the two lineages have maintained substantially independent evolutionary histories.

[Vaquerizas et al. (2025)](/a/73024) reviewed 57 studies published through 2021. Of
those studies, 30 of 36 genetic studies, all four morphological studies, all three
studies of reproduction and growth, four of nine studies of parasites and disease, and
four of five studies of other traits reported a significant difference between Oca and
Occ. Overall, 79% reported at least one significant difference. The principal
non-genetic contrasts were:

- In a biometric sample of 999 rabbits, adult Oca averaged shorter ears (72.76 versus
  78.72 mm), shorter hind feet (56.38 versus 59.45 mm), and lower mass (1,043 versus
  1,234 g). The differences remained after controlling for body mass
  ([Ferreira et al., 2015](/a/73032)). Cranial and postcranial characters also differ in
  frequency, although neither form is reliably identifiable by sight in every case.
- Oca tends to be darker and more finely grizzled. Occ more often has a mixed grey,
  ochre, red, and black agouti appearance. These are tendencies, not invariant
  diagnoses.
- The limited reproductive data indicate smaller litters and earlier sexual maturity in
  Oca. Growth trajectories differ, and maximum dry eye-lens mass, an age-related growth
  measure, is about 15% lower in Oca.
- Some helminths and fleas track one lineage, but many disease-related traits do not.
  Large surveys found similar myxomatosis seroprevalence, and no clear difference in the
  epidemiology or pathology of rabbit hemorrhagic disease.
- Gut microbiomes differ in wild rabbits but not in captive animals, showing that at
  least some reported contrasts are environmental. Recent population trajectories also
  differ: Oca has declined across much of southwestern Iberia while many Occ populations
  have remained stable or increased.

[Villafuerte et al. (2026)](/a/72983) drew the conclusion that Oca should be recognized
as a separate species, _Oryctolagus algirus_. Their paper is a conservation perspective,
not a new delimitation analysis, and it explicitly says that it is not a formal
taxonomic revision. It makes a persuasive case that treating Oca and Occ as separate
species better reflects their evolutionary and conservation status, but it does not
establish that _algirus_ and _cuniculus_ are the correct names for them. Indeed, while
the name _algirus_ obviously refers to Algeria, the paper never refers to North African
rabbits at all.

## Rabbits outside the Iberian Peninsula

The evidence outside Iberia is uneven, but it extends rather than contradicts the
two-lineage model.

**Continental Europe and domestic rabbits.** French rabbits belong to the Occ population
group. [Queney et al. (2001)](/a/73028) found that French genetic diversity is a
bottlenecked subset of northeastern Iberian diversity, consistent with expansion through
or around the Pyrenees. Protein and mitochondrial data place rabbits from France, the
rest of continental Europe, Britain, Australia, and domestic breeds with Occ
([Ferrand & Branco, 2007](/a/73017)). Whole-genome data likewise show that domestic
rabbits derive from wild rabbits of southern France and are closely related to them
([Carneiro et al., 2014](/a/72999)). Thus, even if some introduced populations contain
later admixture, Occ is the source of domestic rabbits and of most of the worldwide
expansion.

**Atlantic islands.** The Azorean populations sampled on São Jorge and Flores cluster
with southwestern Iberian Oca in protein data, and Flores carries Oca lineage-A mtDNA
and southwestern immunoglobulin alleles ([van der Loo et al., 1999](/a/73027);
[Ferrand & Branco, 2007](/a/73017)). Porto Santo is also well supported as Oca. A modern
whole-genome pool of 17 Porto Santo rabbits was included in the data generated by
[Carneiro et al. (2014)](/a/72999), and a reanalysis placed Porto Santo with wild Oca
populations rather than French or domestic Occ
([Rivas-Carrillo et al., 2018](/a/73053)). An independent α-globin study also classified
samples from Flores and Porto Santo as Oca ([Campos et al., 2012](/a/72987)). Published
statements often extend the same assignment to Madeira itself, but the direct
peer-reviewed genetic documentation located here is strongest for the Azores and Porto
Santo; a preliminary study of Madeira appears to have circulated mainly as conference
reports and a dissertation.

Porto Santo is nomenclaturally important because the name _Lepus huxleyi_ Haeckel, 1874
was based on its rabbits. Nineteenth-century authors thought that their small size, dark
color, nocturnal habits, and reported failure to cross with English rabbits had evolved
on the island. [Miller (1912)](/a/5948) instead found the Porto Santo specimens to match
the small Mediterranean form. Modern genetics confirms that Porto Santo and English
rabbits came from opposite Iberian lineages, although island evolution may have added
further local differences.

**North Africa.** Only two North African populations have been genetically connected to
the Iberian lineages with useful precision:

- Ancient and modern rabbits from Zembra, an island off Tunisia, have lineage-B mtDNA,
  the lineage of Occ, northern Spain, southern France, and domestic rabbits. Archaeology
  indicates that rabbits were introduced to Zembra between the Bronze Age and Roman
  period ([Hardy et al., 1994](/a/72994)).
- [Hardy et al. (1995)](/a/72992) included one modern rabbit from Rabat, Morocco. It
  carried mitochondrial haplotype A13, within the Oca lineage.

The status and history of mainland North African rabbits remain uncertain.
[Sen et al. (2024)](/a/67996) interpreted _Oryctolagus_ as a Late Pleistocene human
introduction to the Maghreb. In contrast, [Joleaud (1920)](/a/73055) regarded the rabbit
as indigenous in Berberia rather than a relatively recent introduction.
[Cabrera (1923)](/a/73041), reversing his own earlier belief in a recent introduction,
agreed that North African rabbits had been present for a long time but did not regard
them as African in origin. He interpreted them as a European population that persisted
on land isolated in North Africa by the opening of the Strait of Gibraltar.

Classical morphology does not supply a simple proxy for the genomic lineages.
[Cabrera (1914)](/a/69608) initially used _O. c. algirus_ for the small Mediterranean
rabbit across Iberia, North Africa, and several islands. After collecting more Moroccan
material, [Cabrera (1923)](/a/73041) explicitly changed his mind. He recognized three
Moroccan forms, all different from his central Spanish comparison series: _algirus_ in
the lower Moulouya/eastern Rif and Algerian Tell, _habetensis_ in the Jebala Peninsula,
and _oreas_ around Chefchaouen. His diagnoses emphasized overall size and shades of the
coat: Spanish rabbits were greyer, _habetensis_ browner, _oreas_ blacker, and _algirus_
smaller and more tawny.

## What are the names of the two species?

The present use of _algirus_ for Oca arose through a sequence of provisional
associations rather than a revision of name-bearing material.

[Miller (1912)](/a/5948) recognized a large central European form as _O. c. cuniculus_
and a smaller Mediterranean form as _O. c. huxleyi_, using the Porto Santo name for
rabbits from Spain, the Mediterranean, and the Azores. [Cabrera (1914)](/a/69608) agreed
that there was a small Mediterranean form but called it _algirus_, an older name than
_huxleyi_, because he then thought Spanish and North African rabbits indistinguishable.
Cabrera (1923: 365) withdrew that identification after studying more specimens: he
restricted _algirus_ to the eastern Rif and Algerian Tell and called the Spanish form
_cuniculus_.

The first mtDNA paper was appropriately cautious. Biju-Duval et al. (1991: 100) said
that it was “tempting” to relate their two genetic groups to _O. c. algirus_ and _O. c.
cuniculus_, but that geography was then the **only** argument for the correspondence.
Later work routinely called southwestern lineage A _algirus_ and northeastern lineage B
_cuniculus_. Ferrand & Branco (2007: 220–221), for example, made that assignment while
also acknowledging that the genetic boundaries did not coincide with Cabrera's
morphological distributions.

Cabrera's argument about _cuniculus_ deserves more weight than it has received. He noted
that Linnaeus did not say Germany but “southern Europe”, that wild rabbits apparently
did not occur in Germany in Gesner's time, and that Gesner's information about wild
rabbits came chiefly from Spain. Cabrera concluded that, if a wild population had to be
selected as typical, it should be the Spanish Mediterranean rabbit rather than a German
one. He even treated central Spanish specimens as typical in his 1923 account. This
reasoning creates a real threat to the conventional allocation of _cuniculus_ to Occ.

It does not, however, prove that _cuniculus_ belongs to Oca. Linnaeus's own earlier
account concerned a domestic rabbit of unknown provenance, and domestic rabbits are now
known to derive from Occ. The original description therefore contains signals pointing
in both directions: southern Europe and Spain point toward an Iberian wild population,
while the domestic material points toward Occ. “Germany” is an unsatisfactory
simplification, but replacing it with “Oca” would be another unsupported simplification.
After all, both Oca and Occ occur in Spain.

Of the three principal names, _huxleyi_ is presently the one most directly connected to
a lineage: its name-bearing population is Porto Santo, and modern Porto Santo rabbits
are Oca. This name cannot displace the older _algirus_ if _algirus_ also belongs to Oca,
but it provides a fallback name if the Algerian name proves inapplicable. Conversely, if
a formal typification fixes _cuniculus_ to Oca, the correct name for Occ would have to
be sought among the remaining synonyms. [Wettstein (1927)](/a/73035) used the name
_fodiens_ Gray, 1867, for northern European rabbits, but this name appears to be a
_nomen novum_ for _cuniculus_. Numerous other names are available, though most of the
older ones are based on domesticated rabbits.

## Assessment

My conclusions are:

- There are good reasons to recognize the Oca and Occ lineages as distinct species.
- The name _cuniculus_ is conventionally associated with the Occ lineage. While there is
  some historical reason to doubt this link, in the interest of stability it should be
  preserved. A neotype could be designated for _cuniculus_ to settle the issue.
- The name _algirus_ does have a definite geographic origin (Algiers, Algeria), but it
  is as yet uncertain whether it represents the Oca or Occ lineage. An association with
  the Oca lineage is more geographically plausible but cannot be treated as certain in
  the absence of genetic data.
- If _algirus_ is not available for the Oca lineage, the earliest available name is
  _huxleyi_. It has a definite geographic origin (Porto Santo) and rabbits from the same
  place are genetically linked to Oca.
