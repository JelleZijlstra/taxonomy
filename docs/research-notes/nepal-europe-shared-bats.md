# Are any bat species shared between Nepal and Europe?

_Jelle S. Zijlstra, June 2026_

No bat species is securely established as shared between Nepal and Europe. _Myotis
blythii_ is the strongest candidate, but its name-bearing South Asian population has yet
to be firmly connected with European _oxygnathus_. Most other Nepalese bats formerly
assigned to European species are now treated as separate species.

The starting point is the Nepalese lists in [Csorba et al. (1999)](/a/1695) and
[Dahal et al. (2024)](/a/67356). Csorba et al. listed several species or subspecies with
European names, including _Rhinolophus ferrumequinum tragatus_, _Eptesicus serotinus
pachyomus_, _Nyctalus noctula labiata_, _Myotis blythi blythi_, _Myotis mystacinus
nipalensis_, _Plecotus auritus homochrous_, _Plecotus austriacus wardi_, and
_Miniopterus schreibersii fuliginosus_. Dahal et al. used several updated names, such as
_Myotis nipalensis_, _Plecotus homochrous_, and _Miniopterus fuliginosus_, but also
reported or discussed _Rhinolophus ferrumequinum_, _Nyctalus noctula_, and _Tadarida
teniotis_.

## Candidate species

### _Rhinolophus ferrumequinum_

Nepalese material has often been listed as _R. ferrumequinum tragatus_. Modern
treatments increasingly separate the eastern/Himalayan complex from European _R.
ferrumequinum_; in the western Himalayas, [Saikia et al. (2025)](/a/69679) treat the
comparable material as _R. nippon_ based on mtDNA sequences. _R. nippon_ probably also
occurs in Nepal; Saikia et al. suggest _ferrumequinum_ may reach Kashmir (as the form
_proximus_).

### _Eptesicus serotinus_

The older Nepalese name _Eptesicus serotinus pachyomus_ represents another broad
Palearctic species concept. Current taxonomy limits _serotinus_ to Europe and Central
Asia and treats _pachyomus_ as a separate species, ranging from Afghanistan to South
Korea ([Wilson & Mittermeier, 2019](/a/59249); [Saikia et al., 2025](/a/69679)). This
group was recently moved from _Eptesicus_ to the separate genus _Cnephaeus_
([Cláudio et al., 2023](/a/62645)).

### _Nyctalus noctula_

Nepal is the type area of _labiatus_, historically treated as a synonym of _N. noctula_.
The evidence is reviewed in the note on
[_Nyctalus noctula_, _N. labiatus_, and _N. plancyi_ in Asia](/docs/research-notes/nyctalus-noctula-labiatus-plancyi-asia).
Molecular evidence from China, Vietnam, and the Philippines separates the eastern
_plancyi_/_labiatus_ group from _N. noctula_, but there are still no molecular data from
Nepal.

### _Myotis blythii_

_Vespertilio blythii_ was described from "Nassenabad" in India, later often interpreted
as Nasirabad in Rajasthan. The type-locality evidence for this name, and for _Cnephaeus
pachyomus_, is discussed separately in the note on
[_Cnephaeus pachyomus_ and _Myotis blythii_ type localities](/docs/research-notes/cnephaeus-pachyomus-myotis-blythii-type-localities).
[Topál (1971)](/a/2004) questioned the Rajputana interpretation and synonymized Dobson's
Himalayan _Vespertilio murinoides_ with _M. blythii_.

[Ruedi et al. (2021)](/a/59200) sequenced a Himachal Pradesh specimen, and
[Saikia et al. (2025)](/a/69679) reported that its COI and cytb sequences are close to
Pakistan and Central Asian sequences but about 6% divergent from European _M. b.
oxygnathus_. Earlier molecular work also found Central Asian _blythii_ mitochondria
distinct from European _oxygnathus_ ([Ruedi & Mayer, 2001](/a/50444);
[Furman et al., 2013](/a/11837)).

[Furman et al. (2014)](/a/59392) used mitochondrial and nuclear markers across the
Western Palearctic and found strong cytonuclear discordance. They recommended retaining
broad _M. blythii_ s.l. because European and Central Asian forms were not sufficiently
separated by available nuclear evidence, but they explicitly treated the relationship
between Western Palearctic _blythii_ s.l. and nominal/eastern _blythii_ as a pending
question requiring more sampling east of the Caspian.

### _Myotis mystacinus_

Nepalese _Myotis mystacinus nipalensis_ is now better treated as _Myotis nipalensis_.
[Ruedi et al. (2021)](/a/59200) found Himalayan _M. nipalensis_ to be a distinct lineage
and rejected older placements that tied it to broad Palearctic _M. mystacinus_ or _M.
davidii_.

### _Tadarida teniotis_

[Sharma et al. (2021)](/a/72927) recorded _T. teniotis_ in Nepal based on acoustic data.
[Saikia et al. (2025)](/a/69679) sequenced a western Himalayan Indian specimen
previously treated as _T. teniotis_ and found it strongly divergent from European _T.
teniotis_, but close to Asian _T. insignis_/_T. latouchei_ sequences. They suggested
that the Nepalese record may also represent _T. insignis_.

### _Plecotus auritus_ and _Plecotus austriacus_

Nepalese bats formerly called _Plecotus auritus homochrous_ and _Plecotus austriacus
wardi_ are now recognized as the Himalayan/Asian species _P. homochrous_ and _P. wardi_
([Wilson & Mittermeier, 2019](/a/59249); [Saikia et al., 2025](/a/69679)).

### _Miniopterus schreibersii_

The older name _Miniopterus schreibersii fuliginosus_ reflects the former broad
definition of _M. schreibersii_. South Asian material is now treated as _Miniopterus
fuliginosus_, separate from European _M. schreibersii_
([Wilson & Mittermeier, 2019](/a/59249); [Saikia et al., 2025](/a/69679)).

## South Asian context

[Bates & Harrison (1997)](/a/27903) summarized records of European or western Palearctic
bats in Afghanistan, Pakistan, Kashmir, and northern India. Later revisions include
[Benda & Gaisler (2015)](/a/29223) for Afghanistan and [Saikia et al. (2025)](/a/69679)
for the western Himalayas.

_Rhinolophus hipposideros_ was listed by [Bates & Harrison (1997: 64)](/a/27903) from
Afghanistan, Pakistan, and India, with South Asian localities including Gilgit and
Kashmir. [Benda & Gaisler (2015)](/a/29223) accepted seven Afghan localities, all in
eastern and southeastern Afghanistan, and treated Afghanistan as the southeastern margin
of the species' range. They noted that the range continues east only to Kashmir, with
records from Gilgit and Burnzov cave. They also discussed molecular evidence separating
eastern populations from Europe and the Maghreb by about 3.2-3.8% in mitochondrial
markers, supporting recognition of the eastern subspecies _R. h. midas_. Later,
[Benda et al. (2022)](/a/59672) listed _midas_ as a distinct species restricted to Oman
and Iran. They kept Central Asian and Afghan populations in _R. hipposideros_ but did
not assign them to subspecies. I did not find molecular evidence tying Himalayan
specimens directly to European _R. hipposideros_ in the sources checked here.

_Rhinolophus blasii_ was listed by [Bates & Harrison (1997: 70)](/a/27903) from
Afghanistan and Pakistan. [Benda & Gaisler (2015)](/a/29223) accepted ten Afghan
localities and regarded the Afghan range as the easternmost extension of a mainly
southwestern Palearctic and African species. [Roberts (1977)](/a/72931) reported an
individual collected in Shalimar Gardens in Lahore on 28 December 1968.
[Benda & Gaisler (2015)](/a/29223) noted that the only records farther east than
Afghanistan were from the Lahore area, and treated Afghan material as a distinct eastern
morphotype, _R. b. meyeroehmi_, whose phylogenetic status still needed molecular
testing.

_Myotis daubentonii_ was treated broadly by [Bates & Harrison (1997: 131)](/a/27903),
including records from Kashmir, Afghanistan, Nepal, and Meghalaya. Several South Asian
names formerly associated with _daubentonii_ are now separate species or species
complexes, including _M. longipes_ in the western Himalayas and _M. laniger_ farther
east. [Benda & Gaisler (2015)](/a/29223) did not accept _M. daubentonii_ as an Afghan
species; the Afghan and Kashmir trawling bat in their account is _M. longipes_. They
also noted that the Nepalese material formerly reported as _M. longipes_ had been
revised as _M. csorbai_ (a possible synonym of _M. longipes_ according to
[Ruedi et al. (2021)](/a/59200)), and that the Meghalaya material probably belongs there
too.

_Vespertilio murinus_ was listed by [Bates & Harrison (1997: 158)](/a/27903) from
Pakistan and Afghanistan, with a Pakistan record from Gilgit.
[Benda & Gaisler (2015)](/a/29223) accepted only one verified Afghan record, from
Belchiragh in Faryab Province, and pointed out that some mapped Afghan records in
earlier syntheses were cartographic mistakes derived from that single specimen. They
regarded Afghanistan as part of the southeastern margin of the species' range and
treated the Gilgit record as the only possible record farther east.

_Pipistrellus pipistrellus_ was listed by [Bates & Harrison (1997: 166)](/a/27903)
across a wide area, including Pakistan, Kashmir, Assam, and northern Myanmar.
[Benda & Gaisler (2015)](/a/29223) accepted _P. pipistrellus_ as the most frequently
recorded Afghan bat, with 35 sites, and identified many Afghan specimens previously
reported as _P. coromandra_ as _P. pipistrellus_. They placed the eastern edge of the
range at northern Pakistan and western Kashmir. [Bates et al. (2005:220)](/a/1284)
reidentified the Myanmar record as _P. coromandra_. The Assam record should be treated
as doubtful, and molecular evidence is needed to confirm the identity of bats from
northwestern South Asia.

_Hypsugo savii_ was listed by [Bates & Harrison (1997: 180)](/a/27903), as _Pipistrellus
savii_, from Afghanistan, Meghalaya, and Myanmar. [Benda & Gaisler (2015)](/a/29223)
accepted four Afghan localities, all in the northern mountains, and regarded Afghanistan
as the southern and eastern margin of the known range. They did not accept older records
from Ambala, Gilgit, or Pune as reliable evidence for the species in the Indian
subcontinent, and they stressed that the taxonomic position of Central Asian _H. savii_
still required range-wide molecular testing. [Saikia et al. (2025)](/a/69679) added the
first authenticated western Himalayan and Indian record, from Mandal in Chamoli
district, Uttarakhand. Their specimen was morphologically close to _H. s. caucasicus_
and its COI sequence belonged to a Central Asian _savii_ lineage, but that lineage was
more than 7% divergent from western Palearctic _H. savii_ lineages.

_Nyctalus leisleri_ is the best-supported shared species just outside Nepal.
[Bates & Harrison (1997: 191-192)](/a/27903) listed it from Afghanistan through
Pakistan, Kashmir, Himachal Pradesh, and Uttarakhand. [Benda & Gaisler (2015)](/a/29223)
accepted four Afghan localities and noted that Afghanistan had the greatest density of
records in the Indian-subcontinent part of the range. They interpreted the South Asian
range as a continuous belt along the southern slopes of the Hindu Kush and Himalayas
from eastern Afghanistan to Uttarakhand, separated by a gap of more than 1100 km from
the main western Palearctic range. They found no obvious morphometric separation, but
noted that molecular analyses then available did not sample the Middle East or Indian
subcontinent. [Saikia et al. (2025)](/a/69679) sequenced a Himachal Pradesh specimen and
found it very close to _N. leisleri_ from Uttarakhand, Pakistan, and western Europe:
about 1% K2P in COI and 1.3% in cytb from European samples. They also listed multiple
Uttarakhand records, including Chamoli and Almora districts. I found no Nepalese record
in the sources checked here.

## Sources

- [Csorba et al. (1999)](/a/1695), older Nepal checklist with several European names
  used in broad subspecies concepts.
- [Dahal et al. (2024)](/a/67356), recent Nepal survey and list, including records or
  acoustic data for several candidate names.
- [Bates & Harrison (1997)](/a/27903), older comprehensive South Asian bat treatment,
  useful for historical records of western Palearctic names in South Asia.
- [Benda & Gaisler (2015)](/a/29223), revision of Afghan bat records and taxonomy,
  especially useful for western Palearctic species at South Asia's northwestern edge.
- [Roberts (1977)](/a/72931), Pakistan mammal account, including the Lahore record of
  _Rhinolophus blasii_.
- [Wilson & Mittermeier (2019)](/a/59249), broad current bat classification used here
  for split species such as _Plecotus homochrous_, _P. wardi_, and _Miniopterus
  fuliginosus_.
- [Ruedi et al. (2021)](/a/59200) and [Saikia et al. (2025)](/a/69679), recent Himalayan
  molecular and morphological reassessments.
- [Topál (1971)](/a/2004), discussion of _Myotis blythii_, _murinoides_, and the
  problematic "Nassenabad" type locality.
- [Ruedi & Mayer (2001)](/a/50444), [Furman et al. (2013)](/a/11837), and
  [Furman et al. (2014)](/a/59392), molecular context for the _Myotis blythii_ /
  _oxygnathus_ complex.
